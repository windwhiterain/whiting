**A naming caveat worth recording:** FOC files this species under the Chinese name 巴郎栎, while the
common regional Chinese name in Sichuan/Yunnan is 川滇高山栎. The **Latin binomial is the same in both
cases** — *Quercus aquifolioides* — so the reference set is unambiguous, but a Chinese-language
search for 巴郎栎 will return material that does not appear under 川滇高山栎.

## Site

- **Altitude: 2000–4500 m.** Source: FOC habitat field via
  `https://www.iplant.cn/ashx/getfoc.ashx?key=Quercus%20aquifolioides`
  → "**Montane forests to subalpine scrub; 2000-4500 m.**"
- **Provinces (FOC, verbatim):** Guizhou, W Sichuan, Xizang (Tibet), Yunnan; also Bhutan, Myanmar.
- **Belt:** FOC names **two** belts explicitly — montane forest *and* subalpine scrub. **This is
  therefore the only species in this set that is genuinely two-form**, and the photographs bear this
  out: `01-habit.jpg` is a small upright tree in the montane zone, while `09-habit-scrub-form.jpg`,
  `10`–`16`-series images show a low, dense, sprawling, multi-stemmed shrub on an exposed ridge. The
  upper end of its range (up to 4500 m) is **above** 苍山冷杉's ceiling (4300 m).
- **Grows with:** montane conifers and broadleaved forest at the lower end; high-altitude
  krummholz-type scrub and cushion vegetation at the upper end. *(Estimate/inference — FOC gives no
  community list; the scrub form and its exposed ridge setting are clear in `09-habit-scrub-form.jpg`.)*

## Habit

- **Mature height — FOC, exact:** "Trees **10(-20) m tall**", i.e. typically 10 m, to about 20 m.
- **Trunk diameter:** FOC gives **no diameter figure**. **ESTIMATE: trunk to roughly 0.4–0.6 m d.b.h.**
  Estimated from the trunk-to-height proportion in `01-habit.jpg` and from the multi-stemmed, scrub
  form shown in `09-habit-scrub-form.jpg`. **Marked as an estimate.**
- **Silhouette in one sentence:** a small evergreen oak with a dense, rounded, dark glossy crown of
  leathery, spiny-edged leaves that stands as a neat low tree in forest but collapses into a low,
  sprawling, many-stemmed cushion shrub on exposed ridges.
- **Crown change with age — the requested answer:** **not a conical→flat-topped conifer story at all;
  the dominant change is a change of GROWTH FORM.** In the montane forest it is a **rounded to
  broadly spreading, dense, flat- or domed-crowned small evergreen tree** (see `01-habit.jpg`,
  `02-crown.jpg`); at the upper limit and on exposed sites it becomes a **low, sprawling, multi-stemmed
  scrub shrub** (`09-habit-scrub-form.jpg`). FOC describes no crown shape at all for this species.
  **Modelling should offer a rounded/domed tree form and a separate low multi-stemmed scrub form,
  selected by site, not by age.**

## Branching

- **Whorled or spiral:** oaks are characteristically **spirally (alternate)**, not whorled; the
  photos are consistent with this. *Flagged as an inference — FOC describes only the arrangement of
  leaves, not of branches.*
- **First-order branch attitude:** ascending to horizontal, the crown building as a rounded mass.
  In the scrub form the branches spread along the ground. *Inferred from `01-habit.jpg`, `02-crown.jpg`,
  `09-habit-scrub-form.jpg`.*
- **Lower branches:** **persistent** — this is the single biggest structural difference from the four
  conifers in this study. An oak keeps its lower branches (it does not self-prune into a clean pole
  the way the pines and firs do), and in the scrub form the branches reach the ground entirely.
  `02-crown.jpg` and `09-habit-scrub-form.jpg` both show foliage carried on low branches.
- **Layered or tiered:** **no.** The crown is a continuous, dense, rounded mass of foliage with no
  whorl tiers — the opposite of the tiered pyramidal architecture of the fir and spruce.

## Trunk

- **Straight or contorted:** straight, short, and often **multi-stemmed from low down** in the scrub
  form. *Inferred from `01-habit.jpg`, `09-habit-scrub-form.jpg`.*
- **Base:** not buttressed. *Inferred.*
- **Young branchlets — FOC, exact:** "Young branchlets **pale brown stellate tomentose**" — the young
  shoots are pale brown and covered in a dense, star-shaped (stellate) felt of hairs. This is plainly
  the pale, felted shoot colour visible in `03-branch.jpg`.
- **Bark:** **Flora of China gives no bark description for this species, and PPBC holds no bark
  photograph of it** — I searched the gallery (2863 photographs) and inspected the trunk-bearing
  frames; none shows the trunk bark at usable scale. What the photographs *do* show is the upper
  stems and main limbs as **pale grey and smooth**, seen rising into the canopy in `02-crown.jpg`.
  **I am therefore not supplying a bark reference for this species and not inventing a bark
  description.** For the artist's purposes: treat the bark as unverified, and note that the visible
  limbs above the foliage read as pale grey and smooth.

## Foliage

All figures from FOC via `https://www.iplant.cn/ashx/getfoc.ashx?key=Quercus%20aquifolioides`:

- **Type:** simple broad leaves on a short petiole. **Petiole 2–5 mm, sometimes nearly sessile** — a
  very short petiole, so the leaves sit almost directly against the twig.
- **Length × width: 2.5–7 cm × 1.5–3.5 cm.** Shape: **elliptic to obovate** — obovate means widest
  above the middle and tapering to the base, giving the leaf a distinctly **egg-shaped, broader-toward-
  the-tip** outline that is visible in `04-leaf.jpg`. FOC gives no leaf-retention period in years;
  **ESTIMATE 2–3 years**, typical for a hard-leaved evergreen oak.
- **Margin — FOC, exact:** "**entire or with spiniform teeth**". **Both forms occur on the same
  species, and this is visible in the kept photographs:** `04-leaf.jpg`, `10-leaf-underside-scale.jpg`
  and `06-fruit-acorns.jpg` show leaves with a **smooth, entire, unbroken margin**, while
  `07-acorn-cupule.jpg` and the leaves in `09-habit-scrub-form.jpg` show the **spiniform (spiny,
  sharply pointed) teeth**. **Modelling must support a continuous parameter from entire to spiny.**
- **Apex: obtuse** (blunt, not pointed). **Base: rounded to shallowly cordate.**
- **Venation — FOC, exact:** **6–8 secondary veins on each side of the midrib**; the midrib is
  "apically flexuous" (bending near the tip); tertiary veins obscure beneath. The 6–8 nearly straight,
  parallel-running secondary pairs are clearly countable in `05-leaf-underside.jpg` and
  `10-leaf-underside-scale.jpg`, where they are raised and pale against the rusty indumentum.
- **Surface above — FOC, exact:** "**adaxially glabrescent but pubescent along midvein**" — the upper
  surface becomes hairless with age but keeps hairs along the midrib. The photographs show a
  **strongly glossy, almost wet-looking dark green upper surface** (`04-leaf.jpg`, `06-fruit-acorns.jpg`)
  — that gloss is the first thing that identifies the tree.
- **Surface below — FOC, exact, and this is the strongest single feature:** "densely with
  **reddish brown to orangish brown glandular hairs** (especially abaxially on midvein) when young,
  abaxially with slender **reddish brown to orangish brown stellate hairs and simple hairs or mealy
  scurfy scalelike trichomes with age**". The undersides are therefore a dense **rusty tawny /
  orange-brown felt**, not green. This is unmistakable in `05-leaf-underside.jpg` and
  `10-leaf-underside-scale.jpg`, where the leaf backs are a uniform cinnamon-brown velvety surface.
  **The hard contrast — glossy dark green above, rusty-brown felted below — is the recognition key.**
- **Evergreen: yes — FOC states "evergreen" explicitly.**
- **Terminal rosettes:** no.

## Reproductive

All from FOC via the URL above:

- **Male inflorescence:** long, **pendulous catkins** (the species is monoecious with separate male
  catkins). FOC's numeric description covers the female inflorescence; the catkins are described
  without dimensions. `08-flowers-male-catkins.jpg` and `03-branch.jpg` show them clearly —
  slender, drooping, pale yellow-green catkins.
- **Female inflorescence: 0.5–2.5 cm long**, bearing **1–4 cupules**.
- **Cupule — FOC, exact:** "**shallowly cupular, 5–6 cm × 0.9–1.2 cm**, covering base of nut, outside
  gray pubescent, inside densely tomentose; bracts **ovate-elliptic to lanceolate, apex obtuse and
  often free from the cupule wall**." The cupule is unusually **large relative to the nut** and
  **shallow**, and its bracts stand out free from the wall, producing the fringed, ragged rim clearly
  visible in `06-fruit-acorns.jpg` and `07-acorn-cupule.jpg`. **The cupule is roughly 3× the length
  of the nut it holds** — that disproportion is the obvious field mark.
- **Nut (acorn) — FOC, exact:** "**ovoid to narrowly ovoid, 1.2–2 cm × 1–1.5 cm, glabrous.**" So the
  acorns are 12–20 mm long, 10–15 mm wide, smooth and hairless — visible as the clean green-to-brown
  nuts in `06-fruit-acorns.jpg` and `07-acorn-cupule.jpg`.
- **Position and season:** the acorns are borne in small clusters of **1–4 on short stalks
  (0.5–2.5 cm)** among the leaves. Flowering **May–June**; fruiting **September–October**.

## The five facts that identify this tree from 200 m, as a silhouette

1. **A dense, rounded, dark evergreen crown of a SMALL tree** — this is the only broadleaf in the
   set, and it reads as a compact, rounded, solid mass of dark foliage against the pointed spires of
   the conifers around it. It is never conical.
2. **The crown is carried low, on persistent branches**, not lifted onto a clean pole — the foliage
   comes down the trunk instead of stopping well above the ground.
3. **Above its altitude limit it stops being a tree altogether** and becomes a **low, sprawling,
   many-stemmed cushion shrub** hugging an exposed ridge — a form that no conifer in this set has,
   and which should be a separate model, not a scaled-down tree.
4. **Foliage carried on low branches and twigs in spring, in long drooping pale catkins** — the
   male-flowering tree is draped in pendulous catkins, which gives the crown a distinctly weeping,
   tasselled look (`03-branch.jpg`, `08-flowers-male-catkins.jpg`).
5. **A bright, warm-toned tawny/rusty colour on the leaf undersides**, visible wherever the wind
   turns a leaf — it gives the canopy a bronze cast from below, quite unlike the cold blue-green of
   the fir or spruce.
