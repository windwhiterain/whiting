## Site

- **Altitude: 3000–4300 m.** Source: FOC habitat field via
  `https://www.iplant.cn/ashx/getfoc.ashx?key=Abies%20delavayi`
  → "Mountains; 3000-4300 m."
- **Provinces (FOC, verbatim):** SE Xizang (Tibet), NW Yunnan; also N Myanmar.
  **This is the highest-altitude conifer of the five in this study** (3000–4300 m, against
  2500–4100 m for 丽江云杉 and 1000–3300 m for 华山松).
- **Belt:** **upper subalpine conifer belt**, forming the topmost forest tier on the high massifs —
  the cold, moist upper catchments of the Hengduan Mountains. *("Vegetation belt it dominates" is my
  characterisation; FOC supplies the altitude band only.)*
- **Grows with:** high-mountain conifers, and at its lower edge broadleaved forest. `02-crown.jpg`
  shows it forming a steep, dense, even-aged-looking stand on a mountainside above open montane
  woodland. *(Estimate/inference — FOC gives no community list; read from the photographs.)*

## Habit

- **Mature height / trunk diameter — FOC, exact:** "**Trees to 25 m tall; trunk to 1 m d.b.h.**"
- **Silhouette in one sentence:** a medium-height fir with a dense, sharply tiered, dark blue-green
  pyramidal crown and a short clean trunk, studded with conspicuously **erect, dark blue-black cones**
  standing upright above the foliage.
- **Crown change with age — the requested answer:** **FOC states the crown as "pyramidal"** and the
  stand photographs (`01-habit.jpg`, `02-crown.jpg`) confirm a sharply tiered pyramidal crown in
  ordinary forest-grown trees. **But the age-related change is real and photographically obvious:
  an old tree loses the lower crown, the tiers widen, and the crown becomes broad, open and
  irregular with long, bare, upswept limbs** — this is exactly what `08-habit-old-open-crown.jpg`
  shows, a large old tree with a spreading, open, irregular crown and a visible sinuous trunk.
  **Modelling should interpolate: narrow tiered pyramid (young) → tiered pyramid (forest-grown adult)
  → broad, open, irregular, spreading crown (old / open-grown).** The "juvenile conical → mature
  flat-topped" pattern is *partly* right here, but the mature fir crown is best described as
  **broad and irregular, not cleanly flat-topped.**

## Branching

- **Whorled or spiral:** **whorled**, and the whorl is the visible mechanism of the tiers —
  `01-habit.jpg` shows the tiers plainly, spaced regularly up the trunk. FOC does not state branch
  arrangement. *Flagged as an inference.*
- **First-order branch attitude:** **horizontal to slightly ascending**, forming flat or slightly
  drooping tiers. Very clear in `01-habit.jpg` and `02-crown.jpg`.
- **Lower branches:** **shed**, giving a clean trunk; in the dense stands the bole is bare for much of
  the lower height (`02-crown.jpg`). In the old tree (`08-habit-old-open-crown.jpg`) the self-pruning
  has been very strong and the crown is lifted high.
- **Layered or tiered:** **yes, strongly — this is the species' signature.** The crown is built of
  clearly separated horizontal tiers with gaps of bare trunk between them; each tier ends in upswept,
  densely foliated branch tips (`01-habit.jpg`, `02-crown.jpg`, `07-cone-erect-crown.jpg`).

## Trunk

- **Straight or contorted:** **straight** in forest-grown trees (`01-habit.jpg`, `02-crown.jpg`);
  the old tree in `08-habit-old-open-crown.jpg` shows a distinctly **sinuous, contorted trunk**,
  which is a real feature of old individuals and should be modelled as an age option.
- **Base:** straight, not buttressed. *Inferred from `01-habit.jpg`.*
- **Bark colour and texture — FOC, exact:** "bark **gray-brown, rough, longitudinally splitting**."
  So the bark is **grey-brown, ROUGH, and splits longitudinally** — ridged and furrowed rather than
  plated, and it is **rough** to the touch, which is the character separating it from the plated bark
  of the spruce and the scaly bark of the pines. `03-bark.jpg` shows the trunk and the coarse,
  rough, ridged bark of the old tree's contorted limbs.
- **Branchlets — FOC, exact:** "branchlets initially **red-brown or brown**, then darkened in 2nd or
  3rd year, glabrous, rarely pubescent when young." The reddish-brown young shoots are plainly
  visible in `05-leaf.jpg` and `04-branch.jpg`.
- **Winter buds — FOC, exact:** "winter buds **globose, resinous**" — round and resinous, versus the
  conical/ovoid buds of *Picea*.

## Foliage

All figures from FOC via `https://www.iplant.cn/ashx/getfoc.ashx?key=Abies%20delavayi`:

- **Type:** single needles, borne on a shoot that is **not raised on pegs**.
- **THE DIAGNOSTIC — flat with a notched tip and a suction-cup base, versus *Picea* 4-angled on
  pegs:** FOC gives three separate confirmations, and together they are the cleanest possible
  statement of the *Abies* condition:
  1. **Section:** "**flattened**" — the needle is flat, not four-angled. (*Picea likiangensis* is
     "broadly rhombic in cross section"; the contrast is exact and complementary.)
  2. **Apex:** "**apex emarginate**" — the tip is **notched**, i.e. it has a small V or cleft at the
     end. This is visible in `05-leaf.jpg` and `10-leaf-stomatal-bands.jpg` as the pale, bifid tip.
  3. **Base:** in *Abies* the needle base sits in a **round, depressed suction-cup disc** and the
     needle is **removed leaving a round scar** when it is shed. FOC does not state this for this
     species — it is the genus character — so I mark it as such, and it is visible in
     `04-branch.jpg`, where the foliage lies in a flat plane against the shoot rather than standing
     out radially as in the spruce photograph for comparison.
- **Arrangement — FOC, exact:** "Leaves **spirally arranged, radially spreading ± forward or
  pectinately arranged in 2 lateral sets**". So the needles are either radially/fan-spreading, or
  **swept into two lateral comb-like ranks (pectinate) on either side of the shoot**. The pectinate
  form is what `04-branch.jpg` and `05-leaf.jpg` show, and it gives the shoot its **flat, two-sided,
  comb-like** look — the exact opposite of the spruce's bottlebrush spray.
- **Length × width: 1.5–3 cm × 1.7–2.5 mm.** **Note that the width (1.7–2.5 mm) is nearly as large as
  the lower end of the length.** These are unusually broad, chunky needles for a fir — thick,
  leathery straps rather than slender hairs, and this broadness is plainly visible in `05-leaf.jpg`.
- **Shape:** "often **curved or 'S'-shaped**" — the needles are typically curved into an S when seen
  from the side. `10-leaf-stomatal-bands.jpg` shows this curvature.
- **Margin — FOC, exact:** "margin **strongly revolute**" — the leaf margin is rolled under strongly.
  This is a second *Abies* diagnostic here, and it is very visible in `05-leaf.jpg`, where the thick
  needles have their edges rolled under, exposing the pale underside.
- **Resin canals:** 2, marginal. **Stomatal lines: 2 white bands abaxially** — i.e. **two white
  stripes down the underside of the needle**. This is the fourth *Abies* diagnostic and it is the
  best single close-up feature in `10-leaf-stomatal-bands.jpg` and `05-leaf.jpg`, where the needles
  show a broad silvery-white underside with two paler lines and dark green rolled edges.
- **Terminal rosettes:** no.
- **Evergreen:** yes. Needle persistence **4–6 years** — *ESTIMATE; FOC states no retention period.*
- **Colour above and below — FOC, exact:** "**bright dark green**" above; the **underside is much
  paler**, showing the two white stomatal bands and the rusty indumentum of the young growth. The
  strong above/below contrast (dark glossy green above, silvery-white and rusty below) is very clear
  in `05-leaf.jpg` and is a strong recognition feature when the branch is turned.

## Reproductive

All from FOC via the URL above:

- **Seed cones: 6–11 cm long × 3–4 cm wide**, cylindric or ovoid-cylindric, **shortly pedunculate**.
- **Colour, the key field character:** "**black at maturity, glaucous**". The photographs match this
  exactly — the ripe cones in `06-cone.jpg`, `07-cone-erect-crown.jpg` and `09-cone-cluster.jpg` are
  a striking **dark blue-black / blue-grey, heavily glaucous (bloomed)**, standing upright above the
  green foliage. FOC also records the green immature stage, visible in `09-cone-cluster.jpg`.
- **Seed scales:** flabellate-trapeziform (fan-shaped, four-sided), **1.3–1.5 × 1.4–1.8 cm** — the
  broad fan-shaped scales give the ripe cone its coarse, shield-like surface pattern, which is very
  clear in `06-cone.jpg`.
- **Bracts — FOC, exact:** "**Bracts exserted**, oblong-spatulate, apex with a narrow, usually
  **recurved cusp 3–5 mm**." The **exserted bracts** are the hairlike spines that project from
  between the cone scales; they are unmistakable in `06-cone.jpg` and `09-cone-cluster.jpg`, and no
  other species in this set has them.
- **Seeds:** obovate; wing brown, cuneate-dolabriform (wedge-shaped with a broad rounded end).
- **Orientation and position — THE diagnostic orientation fact:**
  **ERECT and upright**, standing **on the upper crown**, near the tips of the current shoots.
  `07-cone-erect-crown.jpg` shows many of them standing vertically above the foliage like dark
  candlesticks. **This is the exact opposite of *Picea likiangensis*, whose cones hang pendant.**
  Modelling this tree beside the spruce, the cones must point **up**, not down.
- **Season:** pollination **May**; seed maturity **October**.
- **Pollen cones:** not separately dimensioned in the FOC entry; the reddish-brown ones among the
  green shoots in `07-cone-erect-crown.jpg` are them.

## The five facts that identify this tree from 200 m, as a silhouette

1. **A dense, dark blue-green, sharply tiered pyramid**, the tiers separated by visible gaps of bare
   trunk — the crown looks horizontally banded, like a stacked set of plates.
2. **Dark blue-black cones standing upright like candlesticks all over the crown**, projecting above
   the foliage line. Nothing else in this set does this: the spruce's cones hang down, the pines'
   hang or sit subterminally, and the oak's are borne tight against the foliage.
3. **A short clean trunk** with the crown starting high — the species tops out around 25 m, so it
   reads as a squat spire relative to the 50 m spruce standing beside it.
4. **A coarse, rough, spiky foliage texture** — the needles are broad (up to 2.5 mm) and
   strongly revolute-margined, so the foliage surface looks coarse and bristly, not fine and feathery
   like the spruce.
5. **In old trees the crown opens up and goes broad and irregular**, with long bare upswept limbs and
   a visible sinuous trunk — the crown is not permanently a neat spire.
